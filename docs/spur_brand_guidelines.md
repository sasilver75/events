# Spur Brand Guidelines

## 1. Brand Essence

### Brand Name
**Spur**

Spur is a small push toward real life. The name carries several useful meanings:

- **A nudge into motion** — the moment someone decides to leave the house and join something nearby.
- **Spur-of-the-moment energy** — spontaneous, lightweight, happening soon.
- **A catalyst** — one person starts something; others make it real.
- **Local texture** — a spur trail, a side route, a small turn that leads somewhere unexpected.

Spur should feel active without feeling aggressive. It is a prompt, not pressure. It helps people act on a social impulse while making that action feel grounded, safe, and worthwhile.

### Core Idea
**Spur turns nearby intent into real plans.**

People often want to do something, but the cost of organizing is too high and the trust barrier is real. Spur lowers both. It helps someone discover a nearby Event, Commit with confidence, show up, and build local reputation over time.

### Brand Promise
**Real plans with real people, nearby and soon.**

### Brand Personality
Spur is:

- **Local** — rooted in neighborhoods, parks, cafés, gyms, beaches, libraries, campuses, and everyday third places.
- **Human** — warm, social, plainspoken, and lightly playful.
- **Low-friction** — fast to understand, easy to act on, never over-engineered in the user’s face.
- **Accountable** — values showing up, following through, and making strangers feel safer.
- **Spontaneous** — optimized for the next few hours, not someday-maybe planning.
- **Grounded** — energetic but not chaotic; friendly but not juvenile.

Spur should never feel like a dating app, a corporate networking platform, a ticketing marketplace, or a generic events feed.

## 2. Positioning

### Category
**Spontaneous local plans app**

Alternative category language:

- Nearby Events app
- Real-time social planning app
- Local activity discovery app
- Map-based social Events app

Avoid positioning Spur as a “social network.” Spur has social mechanics, but its product goal is action in the real world, not passive content consumption.

### One-Line Description
**Spur helps you find and Commit to nearby Events happening soon — from pickup games to board-game nights — with lightweight trust signals that make meeting new people feel safer.**

### Short App Store Description
**Find something happening nearby, Commit in seconds, and meet people who actually show up. Spur surfaces spontaneous local Events on a map — pickup sports, hikes, games, meetups, and more — with reputation-based trust signals built around follow-through.**

### Longer Product Description
Spur is an iOS app for discovering spontaneous, in-person Events hosted by nearby people. Open the map, find something happening soon, filter by category, and Commit when you’re ready to show up. Spur’s reputation-shaped trust system rewards people who follow through and gently filters out unreliable behavior, helping real-world plans feel safer and easier to join.

### Tagline Options
Primary recommendation:

**Make something happen nearby.**

Strong alternatives:

- **Find the plan. Commit. Show up.**
- **Real plans, right around you.**
- **Your city, in motion.**
- **Nearby plans with people who show up.**
- **Spontaneous plans, safer by design.**
- **Turn free time into a plan.**

Avoid taglines that sound too vague, too dating-oriented, or too startup-polished:

- “Meet your people.” Too dating/social-network coded.
- “Unlock your city.” Too generic.
- “Never be bored again.” Too juvenile and overpromising.
- “Where community happens.” Too institutional.

### Elevator Pitch
Spur is the lowest-friction way to make something happen nearby. It shows spontaneous local Events on a map — a pickup basketball run, a board-game night, a sunrise hike, a tech meetup — and lets people Commit when they’re actually going. Trust comes from behavior: people build reputation by showing up and following through, so meeting new people feels safer than the ambient alternative.

### Strategic Tension
Spur must balance two forces:

1. **Spontaneity** — fast, lightweight, low-pressure.
2. **Trust** — visible enough to create confidence, quiet enough not to make the app feel paranoid.

The brand should make safety feel like infrastructure, not alarm. Users should feel, “This seems reasonable and real,” not “This app thinks everything is dangerous.”

## 3. Audience

### Primary Users
People who are open to doing something soon, nearby, and social, but who do not want the friction of organizing from scratch.

They may be:

- New to a city or neighborhood.
- Socially active but tired of group-chat logistics.
- Looking for pickup sports, casual hobbies, or local meetups.
- Free for the next few hours and open to a plan.
- Comfortable meeting new people, but still want basic trust signals.

### User Motivations
Users come to Spur because they want:

- A plan tonight.
- A reason to leave the house.
- Casual connection without a huge commitment.
- Local activity that feels real, not algorithmic filler.
- A lightweight way to meet people around shared activity.
- Enough trust context to feel comfortable joining.

### User Anxieties
Users may worry:

- “Will anyone actually show up?”
- “Will this be awkward?”
- “Is this safe?”
- “Is this secretly a dating thing?”
- “Will I be trapped in a long commitment?”
- “Is the Event real?”
- “Do I have enough information to go?”

The brand must answer these anxieties through calm product language, visible social proof, and reputation signals based on action.

## 4. Messaging Pillars

### Pillar 1: Real Plans
Spur is about things that are actually happening.

Use language like:

- Happening soon
- Nearby
- Commit
- Show up
- Open spots
- Starts at 6:30
- Real people, real plans

Avoid:

- “Content”
- “Feed”
- “Engagement”
- “Discover experiences”
- “Network with people”

### Pillar 2: Low-Friction Commitment
The main action is not “like,” “save,” or “maybe.” It is **Commit**.

Commitment should feel clear but not intimidating. It means: “I intend to show up.”

Use language like:

- Commit when you’re in.
- Your spot is held.
- Let people know you’re coming.
- Plans work better when people follow through.

Avoid heavy language like:

- Contract
- Obligation
- Penalty
- Violation
- Enforcement

### Pillar 3: Trust Through Follow-Through
Spur’s trust system should be framed around reliability, not surveillance.

Use language like:

- Show-up history
- Follow-through
- Confirmed attendance
- Reliable Committed users
- People who tend to show up
- New user, limited history

Avoid:

- Risk score
- Stranger danger
- Background checked, unless literally true
- Verified safe
- Guaranteed safe
- Trustless
- Policing

### Pillar 4: Local Human Energy
Spur should feel like your neighborhood is quietly alive.

Use language like:

- Around you
- Down the street
- In the next few hours
- Nearby people
- Local plans
- Make the first move
- Start something simple

Avoid language that makes the app feel like an event marketplace:

- Premium experiences
- Curated lifestyle offerings
- Exclusive access
- Tickets
- Venues, unless referring to actual places

## 5. Brand Voice

### Voice Attributes
Spur’s voice is:

- **Plainspoken** — clear before clever.
- **Warm** — human, encouraging, never sterile.
- **Lightly playful** — small sparks of personality, not jokes everywhere.
- **Action-oriented** — verbs over abstractions.
- **Calm about trust** — confident and measured.
- **Locally specific** — names real places and concrete activities.

### Voice Formula
**Clear action + local context + human warmth.**

Examples:

- “Three people are Committed. One more makes it a game.”
- “Exact spot unlocks after you Commit.”
- “Maya usually shows up when she Commits.”
- “This Event starts in 42 minutes.”
- “Lightweight plan. Real people. Nearby.”

### Writing Rules

#### Use verbs.
Good:
- “Commit to join.”
- “Start an Event.”
- “Bring a friend.”
- “Show up and confirm.”

Weak:
- “Event participation is available.”
- “Users may indicate attendance.”
- “Engagement opportunities exist nearby.”

#### Keep copy concrete.
Good:
- “Pickup volleyball at Santa Monica beach.”
- “Board games at a café in Los Feliz.”
- “Sunrise hike near Griffith.”

Weak:
- “A social experience in your area.”
- “A unique opportunity to connect.”

#### Make trust feel normal.
Good:
- “People build reputation by showing up.”
- “New Event from a user with limited history.”
- “Most people who Committed are confirmed regulars.”

Weak:
- “We protect you from dangerous strangers.”
- “This user has been algorithmically scored.”
- “Our safety system guarantees trust.”

#### Avoid over-social promises.
Good:
- “Find something to do nearby.”
- “Meet people through the plan.”
- “A simple way to join in.”

Weak:
- “Find your tribe.”
- “Make lifelong friends tonight.”
- “The future of human connection.”

### Tone by Context

#### Map / Discovery
Tone: immediate, inviting, useful.

Examples:
- “Happening near you.”
- “Starts soon.”
- “2 spots left.”
- “Open to new people.”

#### Event Detail
Tone: specific, confidence-building.

Examples:
- “Exact location appears after you Commit.”
- “5 of 6 Committed.”
- “Maya has a strong show-up history.”
- “Bring water. Casual pace.”

#### Commitment Flow
Tone: clear, accountable, friendly.

Examples:
- “Commit only if you expect to show up.”
- “Plans work better when people can count on each other.”
- “You’re in. We’ll help you get there.”

#### Cancellation
Tone: non-shaming but firm.

Examples:
- “Can’t make it? Release your spot so someone else can join.”
- “Early cancellations are okay. No-shows make plans harder.”
- “Thanks for letting the group know.”

#### Safety / Trust
Tone: calm, direct, never alarmist.

Examples:
- “Meet in public places when possible.”
- “Trust signals are based on follow-through, not popularity.”
- “Report behavior that makes the Event feel unsafe.”

## 6. Terminology

### Product Terms
Use these terms consistently.

| Term | Meaning | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Event** | A spontaneous in-person plan on Spur | Capitalize when referring to product object. |
| **Commit** | Primary action to join an Event | Use instead of RSVP when possible. |
| **Committed** | A user has committed to attend | “5 Committed” is acceptable. |
| **Host** | The person who created the Event | Familiar and user-facing. |
| **Spot** | Available place in an Event | Useful for capacity. |
| **Show-up history** | Reputation based on follow-through | Human-readable trust language. |
| **Nearby** | Local enough to reasonably attend soon | Core brand word. |
| **Happening soon** | Event is within the next few hours | Better than “real-time.” |

### Terms to Avoid or Use Carefully

| Avoid | Prefer | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| RSVP | Commit | RSVP feels passive and event-platform coded. |
| Feed | Map, Events, nearby Events | Feed implies passive scrolling. |
| Stranger | New people, nearby people | “Stranger” is useful internally, but can feel unsafe in user copy. |
| User score | Show-up history, reliability signal | Avoids cold gamification. |
| Reputation score | Reputation, show-up history | “Score” can feel judgmental if overused. |
| Host rating | Host history | Less marketplace-like. |
| Penalty | Impact, reduced visibility, release spot | Avoids punitive tone. |
| Safe / guaranteed safe | Safer, more confidence, trust signals | Never overpromise safety. |
| Social network | Local plans app | Spur should be action-first. |

### Capitalization

- **Spur**: Always capitalized.
- **Event**: Capitalize when referring to the Spur product object.
- **Commit / Committed**: Capitalize when referring to the product action or state.
- **Host**: Capitalize when referring to the Event creator role.
- Category names should be title case in UI labels: **Sports**, **Games**, **Outdoors**, **Food**, **Meetups**, **Study**, **Music**.

## 7. Visual Identity

### Visual Direction
Spur’s visual identity should feel like a warm, animated city map: lightweight, local, and alive. It should borrow the friendliness of illustrated city guides and the immediacy of map-based games, without becoming cartoonish or childish.

The app should feel:

- Native to iOS.
- Warm and playful.
- Map-first.
- Human-scale.
- Slightly kinetic.
- Trustworthy without being institutional.

### Visual Keywords

- Warm city
- Nearby motion
- Soft urgency
- Human geography
- Small sparks
- Friendly commitment
- Spontaneous routes
- Real-world plans

### Moodboard References
Use these as directional references, not literal copies:

- Animated city-guide illustrations.
- Pokémon Go’s sense of a living nearby map.
- Apple’s native iOS polish and spacing discipline.
- Soft editorial travel illustration.
- Neighborhood posters, pickup-game flyers, hand-drawn route maps.
- Sun-warmed Los Angeles palettes: peach, terracotta, sage, cream, soft charcoal.

Avoid:

- Corporate blue SaaS identity.
- Dating-app gloss and gradients.
- Club/nightlife black-and-neon.
- Ticketmaster/Eventbrite marketplace feel.
- Default Google Maps or Apple Maps cartography.
- Overly gamified badges and XP language.

## 8. Logo System

### Logo Concept
The Spur logo should suggest motion, locality, and a gentle nudge.

Recommended conceptual directions:

1. **Map Pin + Spark**
   - A softened map pin with a small spur/spark cut or notch.
   - Communicates local discovery and activation.

2. **Route Spur**
   - A main path with a small branching route.
   - Communicates spontaneous side quests and nearby plans.

3. **S Monogram in Motion**
   - A rounded “S” shaped like a path or trail.
   - Simple enough for app icon use.

4. **Commit Mark**
   - A small check, spark, or notch integrated into the icon.
   - Signals follow-through without looking like task management.

### Logo Personality
The mark should be:

- Rounded but not bubbly.
- Simple enough to survive at app-icon size.
- Warm rather than techy.
- Slightly directional, as though something is beginning.

### Logo Construction Guidance

- Use a rounded geometric base.
- Avoid sharp aggressive spurs that imply cowboy boots, weapons, or sports teams.
- Avoid literal horse spurs unless stylized beyond recognition.
- Avoid compass clichés unless made unusually simple.
- The icon should work as a filled silhouette.

### Wordmark
Recommended wordmark style:

- Custom or semi-custom rounded sans-serif.
- Slightly condensed proportions acceptable.
- Friendly terminals.
- Lowercase option: **spur** feels casual and approachable.
- Titlecase option: **Spur** feels more brand-stable and app-store appropriate.

Primary recommendation: **Spur** in title case for brand and App Store; lowercase **spur** may be used in expressive campaign graphics.

### Clear Space
Maintain clear space around the logo equal to the height of the “u” in the wordmark or one quarter of the icon height, whichever is larger.

### Minimum Size

- App icon: standard iOS adaptive sizes.
- Standalone logo mark: no smaller than 24px.
- Wordmark: no smaller than 72px wide in digital contexts.

### Logo Don’ts

Do not:

- Add drop shadows to the core logo.
- Rotate the mark arbitrarily.
- Use neon gradients.
- Put the logo over busy map details without a solid backing.
- Stretch or compress the wordmark.
- Use a horse spur illustration as the primary mark.
- Make the icon look like a navigation app, delivery app, or fitness tracker.

## 10. Typography

### Typography Direction
Typography should feel native, legible, and human. Spur is a mobile utility with personality, so type must prioritize clarity at small sizes.

### Primary Typeface
**SF Pro**

Use the native iOS system typeface for product UI. It gives Spur polish, speed, accessibility, and platform familiarity.

### Brand / Marketing Typeface Options
For marketing materials, landing pages, or campaign graphics, pair SF Pro with one of these directions:

1. **Rounded Humanist Sans**
   - Friendly, accessible, local.
   - Examples by style: Avenir Next Rounded, Nunito Sans, Satoshi, Circular-like systems.

2. **Soft Geometric Sans**
   - Modern but not corporate.
   - Examples by style: Inter Tight, Manrope, Geist, Plus Jakarta Sans.

3. **Editorial Accent Serif, Used Sparingly**
   - Adds warmth for neighborhood-guide moments.
   - Use only for campaign headlines or printed local posters.

### Type Scale for iOS UI

| Role | Size | Weight | Usage |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Large Title | 34 | Bold | Major screens, onboarding |
| Title 1 | 28 | Bold | Event title, section headers |
| Title 2 | 22 | Semibold | Modal headers |
| Headline | 17 | Semibold | Buttons, important labels |
| Body | 17 | Regular | Main copy |
| Callout | 16 | Regular / Semibold | Pills, metadata |
| Subhead | 15 | Regular | Secondary text |
| Footnote | 13 | Regular | Trust explanations, details |
| Caption | 12 | Medium | Category labels, badges |

### Typography Rules

- Keep Event titles direct and scannable.
- Use sentence case for most UI labels.
- Use title case for categories and main navigation labels.
- Avoid all caps except tiny badges, and use sparingly.
- Prefer weight and spacing over extra color for hierarchy.

## 11. Illustration Style

### Illustration Direction
Illustration should make Events feel approachable and specific. Use simple scenes, objects, and neighborhood cues rather than generic social illustrations.

### Style Attributes

- Flat or lightly dimensional 2D.
- Rounded geometry.
- Warm sunlight and soft shadows.
- Textured but not noisy.
- People shown as simple, inclusive figures.
- Activity-specific props: basketball, hiking trail, game pieces, laptop, coffee cup, guitar.
- Background details should suggest place without requiring exact realism.

### Illustration Subjects

- A basketball hoop at a local court.
- A blanket with board games on a café table.
- A trailhead at sunrise.
- A small meetup table with laptops and coffee.
- A picnic table in a park.
- A beach volleyball net.

### Illustration Don’ts

Do not use:

- Corporate blob people.
- Overly glossy 3D characters.
- Dating-style portraits.
- Generic confetti celebration art.
- Fake futuristic cityscapes.

## 12. Map Style

### Map Philosophy
The map is not just navigation. It is the emotional center of Spur. It should make the user feel that nearby life is available right now.

### Map Visual Direction

- Custom-themed 2D map with slight tilted perspective where appropriate.
- Warm land tones, soft green parks, muted roads.
- Minimal labels, only useful local anchors.
- Pins should feel animated and alive without cluttering the map.
- Avoid default Apple Maps / Google Maps visual language.

### Map Layers

Primary layers:

1. **Base geography** — roads, parks, water, neighborhoods.
2. **Event pins** — category-coded, capacity-aware.
3. **User location** — present but not dominant.
4. **Time sensitivity** — subtle pulse for Events starting soon.
5. **Cluster states** — grouped pins by density.

### Pin System

Pins should communicate:

- Category.
- Event vitality.
- Capacity or momentum.
- Trust confidence when relevant.

Recommended pin structure:

- Rounded map pin or pill.
- Category icon inside.
- Small capacity ring or dot cluster.
- Optional shimmer/pulse for Events close to activation threshold.

### Pin States

| State | Visual Treatment |
|---|---|
| New Event | Soft peach pin with category icon |
| Nearly full | Marigold rim or small glow |
| Committed by user | Sage check or filled state |
| Starting soon | Gentle pulse, never frantic |
| Full | Muted pin, still visible but less prominent |
| Low trust / limited history | Neutral treatment with explanatory label in detail view, not alarming map styling |

### Map Don’ts

- Do not overuse pins until the map looks like a notification explosion.
- Do not use harsh red for urgency unless there is a true warning.
- Do not make trust status the dominant map signal.
- Do not make the user feel tracked or exposed.

## 13. UI Design System

### UI Principles

1. **Map first, details second.**
   The user should see what is happening around them immediately.

2. **Commitment is clear.**
   The primary action should always be obvious.

3. **Trust is visible at the decision point.**
   Show trust context on Event detail screens, commitment flows, and user profiles.

4. **Friction appears only where it earns its keep.**
   Add friction for safety, accountability, or clarity — not for ceremony.

5. **Everything should feel like it can happen soon.**
   Time, distance, capacity, and commitment status should be prominent.

### Core Screens

#### Map Home
Must show:

- Current area.
- Event pins.
- Category filters.
- Search or neighborhood selector.
- Quick create button.
- Time horizon filter, such as “Now,” “Tonight,” “Tomorrow.”

Primary feeling: “There are real possibilities near me.”

#### Event Detail Sheet
Must show:

- Event title.
- Category illustration or hero.
- Time and duration.
- Fuzzed location before Commit.
- Exact location after Commit.
- Host identity.
- Commitment count and capacity.
- Trust / show-up context.
- What to bring or know.
- Primary Commit button.

Primary feeling: “I understand what this is, who started it, and what happens if I join.”

#### Commit Flow
Must show:

- Commitment confirmation.
- Reminder that Commit means intending to show up.
- Cancellation guidance.
- Location reveal behavior.
- Calendar / notification option.

Primary feeling: “I’m in, and the app will help me follow through.”

#### Create Event
Must show:

- Activity title.
- Category.
- Time and duration.
- General location.
- Capacity.
- Description / notes.
- Public-place reminder.
- Host commitment expectations.

Primary feeling: “I can start something without becoming an event planner.”

#### Profile / Reputation
Must show:

- Name and photo.
- Short bio or interests.
- Show-up history.
- Events hosted / joined.
- Mutuals or shared activity history when available.
- Trust context explained in plain language.

Primary feeling: “This person has enough context for me to make a decision.”

### Component Style

#### Buttons

Primary CTA:
- Filled Spur Peach.
- Charcoal label.
- Rounded 16–20px radius.
- Haptic confirmation on tap.

Secondary CTA:
- Cream or Sand surface.
- Charcoal label.
- Subtle border.

Destructive CTA:
- Clay Red text or border.
- Avoid full red fills unless the action is severe.

Button copy examples:

- Commit
- Release spot
- Start Event
- Invite a friend
- Show exact spot
- Confirm arrival

#### Cards

Cards should feel like warm paper surfaces, not SaaS panels.

- Background: Warm Cream or Soft Sand.
- Radius: 20–28px.
- Shadow: soft, low opacity.
- Border: subtle warm gray.
- Padding: generous, 16–24px.

#### Pills

Use pills for compact metadata:

- “Tonight 6:30 PM”
- “2.1 mi away”
- “5 of 6 Committed”
- “Public place”
- “Casual pace”
- “Bring water”

#### Trust Badges

Trust badges should be understated. They are context, not trophies.

Examples:

- Strong show-up history
- New to Spur
- Confirmed regular
- Hosted 3 Events
- Usually on time

Avoid gamified labels like:

- Elite
- Legendary
- Level 8
- Top 1%
- Power Host

## 14. Trust & Safety Brand Guidelines

### Trust Positioning
Spur cannot and should not promise that meeting new people is risk-free. The brand should instead communicate that Spur improves the baseline by making intent, history, and follow-through more visible.

Recommended framing:

**Spur makes local plans feel more accountable by showing who is Committed, how people follow through, and what to expect before you go.**

### Trust Principles

1. **Behavior over popularity.**
   Reputation should come from showing up, not collecting likes.

2. **Explain the signal.**
   Users should understand why a trust indicator exists.

3. **Do not shame people.**
   Missed plans happen. Patterns matter.

4. **Protect privacy.**
   Show enough to build confidence, not enough to expose someone unnecessarily.

5. **Prefer public-place defaults.**
   Encourage Events in public or semi-public settings, especially for early versions.

### Trust Copy Examples

Good:

- “Alex has shown up for most Events they’ve Committed to.”
- “New to Spur. Limited show-up history.”
- “Exact location is shared after you Commit.”
- “This Event is in a public place.”
- “Release your spot if plans change.”
- “Reports help keep Events useful and respectful.”

Avoid:

- “Alex is safe.”
- “Verified trustworthy.”
- “Risk-free meetup.”
- “We screen everyone.”
- “Bad users are punished.”

### Reputation Language

Preferred phrase: **show-up history**

Support terms:

- Follow-through
- Confirmed arrival
- Released spot early
- No-show pattern
- Host history

Avoid exposing raw numerical scores too prominently in early UI. Use tiers or explanations where possible.

Recommended tiers:

- **New to Spur** — limited history.
- **Building history** — some follow-through data.
- **Strong show-up history** — reliable pattern.
- **Very strong show-up history** — consistently reliable.

### Cancellation Language

Spur should treat cancellations as normal when handled responsibly.

Examples:

- “Plans changed? Release your spot.”
- “Early release keeps your history healthy.”
- “No-shows make Events harder for everyone.”
- “Thanks for freeing up a spot.”

Avoid:

- “You failed to attend.”
- “Penalty applied.”
- “Your reputation has been damaged.”

### Safety Disclaimers

Use clear, direct disclaimers in safety surfaces:

- “Use your judgment when meeting new people.”
- “Prefer public places, especially for first-time Events.”
- “Leave or report anything that feels unsafe.”
- “Spur provides trust signals, not guarantees.”

## 15. Motion & Interaction

### Motion Personality
Motion should feel like small sparks of local activity. It should help users understand what changed, not perform for attention.

### Motion Principles

- Quick, soft, and purposeful.
- Use spring physics sparingly.
- Favor subtle pulses for nearby activity.
- Celebrate Commit with a small confirmation, not confetti overload.
- Respect Reduce Motion settings.

### Recommended Motion Moments

#### Event Pin Appears
A pin rises gently from the map with a tiny bounce.

#### Event Nearly Tips / Fills
Use a soft shimmer or capacity ring animation.

#### Commit Confirmation
Button compresses, then transforms into “Committed” with a sage check.

#### Location Reveal
Fuzzed location pill expands into exact location after Commit.

#### Arrival Confirmation
Small haptic, checkmark, and “You’re here” state.

### Motion Don’ts

- No slot-machine animations.
- No aggressive countdown shaking.
- No dopamine-heavy streak explosions.
- No safety warnings that animate like emergency alerts unless truly urgent.

---

## 16. Sound & Haptics

### Sound
Default product should rely on haptics, not sound. If sound is used, it should be optional and minimal.

Recommended sound profile:

- Warm click.
- Soft map pop.
- Gentle confirmation tone.

Avoid:

- Casino-like sounds.
- Notification pings that feel stressful.
- Game achievement fanfare.

### Haptics

Use haptics for:

- Commit confirmation.
- Releasing a spot.
- Arrival confirmation.
- Pin selection.
- Event creation.

Haptics should be light or medium, never harsh.

## 24. Naming Patterns for Events

### Event Title Formula
**Activity + place/context + useful qualifier**

Examples:

- Pickup basketball — Echo Park courts
- Board games at Stories Café
- Sunrise hike — Griffith Observatory
- Coffee and indie hacking — Silver Lake
- Casual volleyball — Santa Monica beach
- Chess in the park — beginner friendly

### Good Event Titles

- Specific.
- Human.
- Short enough to scan.
- Clear about activity.
- Clear enough about place before exact location reveal.

### Weak Event Titles

- “Hangout”
- “Fun night”
- “Meet people”
- “Vibes”
- “Cool event”
- “Networking opportunity”

### Event Description Pattern

Use this structure:

1. What is happening.
2. Who it is for.
3. What to bring or know.
4. Tone / difficulty.

Example:

“Casual beach volleyball for mixed skill levels. We’ll play doubles or rotate depending on turnout. Bring water and shoes you can get sandy. Friendly pace, no tournament energy.”

## 26. Do / Don’t Summary

### Do

- Make Spur feel like the city is alive nearby.
- Use concrete Event examples.
- Treat Commit as the central action.
- Make trust visible but calm.
- Use warm colors and native iOS polish.
- Keep copy short, direct, and human.
- Reward follow-through without turning the app into a game.
- Encourage public, understandable Events.

### Don’t

- Sound like a dating app.
- Sound like a corporate networking app.
- Overpromise safety.
- Use fear-heavy stranger language.
- Make reputation feel like a credit score.
- Hide basic Event details behind clever UX.
- Make the map look like default navigation software.
- Use generic social-network language.
- Turn every interaction into gamification.

## 28. Initial Launch Guidance

### v0 Brand Focus
For an early personal / learning version, prioritize:

1. Clear Event discovery.
2. Commit / release spot flow.
3. Basic show-up history.
4. Public-place defaults.
5. Warm map-first identity.
6. Trust copy that does not overpromise.

Defer high-scale brand complexity:

- Full reputation tiers.
- Heavy moderation language.
- Complex community programs.
- Rich achievement systems.
- Overbuilt creator/Host branding.

### Upgrade Before Wider Distribution
Before a broad public launch, strengthen:

- Safety guidelines.
- Reporting and moderation surfaces.
- Abuse-prevention copy.
- Privacy explanations.
- Location sharing controls.
- Reputation transparency.
- Crisis / emergency guidance.
- Brand review for vulnerable user scenarios.

## 29. Final Brand Summary

Spur is a warm, map-first iOS app for making nearby plans happen. Its brand should feel local, spontaneous, and human, with a product system built around commitment and follow-through. The central emotional promise is simple: open Spur, find something real nearby, Commit with confidence, and show up.

The brand wins when it makes spontaneous social action feel easier without making trust feel heavy. It should help users feel that their city is full of small, reachable possibilities — and that the people joining them are also choosing to show up.
